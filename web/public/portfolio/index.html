Getting to a building and finding what is inside it are two different problems. CalShi solves the second one: which floor, which room, whether it is accessible, and whether it is working right now. This is the whole design process that got us there — eight interviews to a deployed app, in order.

The problem

The last hundred feet

Standard mapping tools guide you across campus and stop at the entrance. They do not show the location or the condition of anything inside.

That gap is most frustrating in exactly the situations where you can least afford it — when you need a restroom, a water refill station, a vending machine, or somewhere to sit and work, in a building you have never been in before. Locked doors, broken fixtures, and unmarked rooms make the guesswork worse.

So we started where we should have: by asking people how they actually cope today.

01

Needfinding

Eight interviews, and one thing nobody could answer

We interviewed eight campus users about how they find amenities and where that goes wrong.

To keep the picture wide, we recruited students at different stages of their degree — some we knew, some at random — plus a UC Berkeley professor and a staff member from the Tang Center. Between them they covered frequent and infrequent campus users, people who knew particular buildings well and people who did not, and people who regularly need restrooms, water, food, and study space. No participant had taken CS 160, which kept the feedback honest.

We asked for background on which amenities they use and how often, then pushed hard on the friction: what is frustrating, what they work around, and what they did the last time it went badly. We also asked each participant to find a nearby restroom, water fountain, vending machine, or study spot from where we were sitting, and to narrate it.

The interview protocol — all 16 questions
  1. How many times a week are you on the UC Berkeley campus?
  2. What areas or buildings on campus do you usually spend time in?
  3. What campus amenities do you find yourself using, or looking for the most (bathroom / water fountain / vending machine / study spot)?
  4. What does your experience of using these usually look like for you?
  5. Are there any challenges, frustrations, or workarounds you commonly experience?
  6. From where we are now, show us how you would find a nearby bathroom / water fountain / vending machine / study spot.
  7. Why did you do it that way?
  8. How did you feel during the task?
  9. Was anything confusing, frustrating, or difficult?
  10. Was there any moment where you felt unsure?
  11. Was this your usual method, or did you take a new approach for this instance?
  12. Can you tell me about a time when finding what you were just looking for was especially frustrating?
  13. Do you think an application that shows you the locations of the amenities would help solve this problem?
  14. Are there any other challenges regarding campus amenities you want to mention?
  15. What solutions would resolve these challenges?
  16. Is there anything else you would like to add?

What we heard

Memory is the map

People navigate to amenities they have already used. It works — right up until they are somewhere unfamiliar.

Otherwise, wander

Outside familiar territory the fallbacks are wandering, following signs, or asking whoever is nearby.

Some amenities are invisible

Water refill stations and vending machines were hardest of all to discover, with no indoor map or indicator to point at them.

Condition decides

Crowding and cleanliness — restrooms especially — made people unwilling to use a facility even when they knew where it was.

The five questions we designed against

  1. How might we help campus users find nearby amenities in unfamiliar areas without requiring prior knowledge of the campus?
  2. How might we help users navigate to amenities located inside complex or unfamiliar campus buildings?
  3. How might we communicate whether an amenity is currently open, accessible, crowded, or out of service?
  4. How might we help users quickly find an alternative when their usual amenity is unavailable?
  5. How might we make less-visible campus resources, such as water refill stations, vending machines, and study spaces, easier to discover?

→ What this changed

Question three became the spine of the product. Everything that followed had to answer is this thing open, reachable, and working before it answered where is it.

02

Lo-fi prototyping

Three ways to solve it, then two ways to build it

We deliberately generated concepts that were different in kind, not in detail — a phone, a building, and a wrist.

  1. A campus amenity map on your phone

    A map of campus with amenities marked and searchable. Pull out your phone, let GPS place you, and route to the nearest one you need.

  2. Smart signs at every building entrance

    A display at the door listing what the building has, whether it is available, and how to get to it — with a QR code for anyone who wants the detail on their phone.

  3. A smartwatch that notices before you do

    Biometrics detect that you are dehydrated or need a break, and the watch surfaces the three nearest options with directions — before you think to search.

Why we chose the phone

The signs would reach people without a smartphone, and the watch would catch a need before it became urgent — both genuinely better on one axis. But the map app was the only one that was immediately familiar to anyone who has used Google or Apple Maps, needed no new campus infrastructure, and raised no biometric-privacy questions. We took Concept A forward.

Two paper prototypes, argued in opposite directions

Rather than refine one idea, we built two architectures and let users break them.

Prototype A

Map-first

Start from your current location, pick or search an amenity type, then compare individual amenity markers geographically. Reviews live inside the location-selection flow, so proximity and community knowledge drive the choice.

Prototype B

Building-first

Start from a list of nearby buildings, pick a building, pick a floor, then browse what is on it. More explicit indoor structure and a dedicated “Add Amenity” form — but no individual amenities on the campus map to begin with.

What we asked people to do

  1. Find a nearby amenity
  2. Evaluate and locate the best amenity
  3. Contribute amenity information

These three tasks stayed fixed for the rest of the project, which is why the numbers in Milestone 05 are directly comparable to this round.

What worked

  1. Users understood each prototype’s primary browsing structure.
  2. Prototype B’s building-first structure was understandable on its own terms.
  3. Reviews genuinely helped users evaluate amenities.
  4. Users saw value in community-maintained information — but wanted safeguards.

What broke

Usability issues from paper-prototype testing, rated by severity.
Sev.IssueEvidence
3–4 Search scope was inconsistent and ambiguous P5 searched “bathroom”, but Prototype B only searched building names. One field has to clearly cover both amenity and building queries.

2–3 Reporting and contribution actions were unclear P1–P3 wanted to report false reviews, P4 could not find “add amenity”, P5 did not notice reviews, and P6 expected to report a dirty bathroom through a review.

→ What this changed

Neither prototype won outright, so we merged them: keep A’s amenity-based map search, adopt B’s building-and-floor organisation for indoor detail, and make one search field cover both. The interface also had to say plainly what is searchable, whether an amenity is worth the walk, who is allowed in, and how community submissions get verified.

03

Hi-fi prototyping

Three visual answers to the same screen

Before designing the whole app, three of us independently designed one screen — amenity search results — and then argued about them against Nielsen-style heuristics.

We synthesised instead of picking a winner

Style A became the foundation, because it encoded the availability signal that needfinding and paper testing had both flagged as the thing that decides whether a trip is worth making. Then we explicitly grafted on Style B’s category filters and map markers, which covered A’s biggest gap: spatial context and fast filtering.

Then we tested the whole app on strangers

We built a fully interactive high-fidelity prototype covering search, map, building and amenity detail, and the contribution flows, and ran it past two participants from outside the team and outside CS 160. Three themes came back.

  1. The visual design read as clear and familiar

    “Straightforward, easy familiarity.”

    P2

  2. The primary task was understandable

    “I think it’s clear and intuitive to use.”

    P1

  3. Information needed better prioritisation

    “Highlight the available ones… or show at the end.”

    P2

    Both flagged redundant or badly ordered information, including duplicated accessibility text.

Naming it, and planning the build

We named the application CalShi and wrote the tagline it still carries: find the campus amenities you need, fast. Alongside the shipped 2D direction we ran an exploratory second prototype — a 3D rendered campus model with amenity data attached, covering 253 buildings and 313 amenities — which was never meant to ship but told us what scale of data the real product would have to carry.

For the implementation sprint we split a conservative task set (the five required pages, authentication, persistent data) from stretch goals (map-based routing, submission moderation, preference-based ordering), assigned work by skill set, and deliberately gave the shared infrastructure — the data model and Firebase setup — to a single person so integrations would not collide.

→ What this changed

Two decisions carried into implementation: keep the colour system and interaction conventions exactly as tested, and treat availability-first ordering as a requirement rather than a nicety. We kept the first promise. Milestone 04 is where we admit what happened to the second.

04

Implementation

From hard-coded screens to real campus data

CalShi shipped as a deployed web application backed by Firebase for authentication and persistent storage — replacing every hard-coded value in the hi-fi prototype with real, admin-curated and user-generated content.

What we built

Accounts

Google sign-in, with preferences, reviews, and submissions linked to the account and following the user across devices.

Search and filtering

Across buildings and amenity types, ordered by distance, and re-prioritised by a saved restroom-category preference when one is set.

Live campus map

The user’s current location, building markers, and a walking route generated automatically to whichever building they choose.

Indoor detail

Floor, room or location description, accessibility, hours, and an availability badge: available, out of service, or unknown.

Contribution loop

A validated “Add amenity” form saved privately pending manual review, plus public 1–5 star reviews with comments.

Five distinct states

Home and search, campus map, building detail, amenity detail, and account settings.

Getting the data was the hard part

There is no single source for what is inside UC Berkeley’s buildings. We merged three independently maintained ones — a campus navigation app’s internal dataset, a university facilities GIS layer, and the sustainability office’s public asset map — none of which shared an identifier scheme. The result links 242 buildings to 1,296 amenity records across 9 categories.

Restrooms, the seed dataset

The public campus restroom map turned out to be a thin client over a facilities ArcGIS FeatureServer: it did no computation, and simply rendered two feature layers — “Restrooms by Building” and “Building not yet surveyed”. So instead of scraping rendered output we queried the same REST endpoint directly and got structured attributes: room numbers, per-category counts, and free-text notes about changing tables and menstrual-product dispensers.

Two obstacles showed up immediately. The API’s default response size truncated large field lists mid-record, and asking for building polygon geometry alongside attributes pushed responses past the size ceiling. Requesting attributes and geometry in separate passes, and generalising the polygon coordinates, brought each full layer back inside a single response. That produced 242 buildings, 177 of which carry 1,013 restrooms; the other 65 have not been surveyed. Every later amenity was matched onto this building list.

Everything else, and a truncation that nearly slipped through

A public Leaflet map publishing six GeoJSON layers was the only source with real coverage of water refill stations, lactation rooms, microwaves, zero-waste stations, eateries, campus gardens, and basic needs services. Because it renders client-side, the data files are not in the page source at all — we found the endpoints by inspecting the scripts the live page loads.

Four layers fetched cleanly. The self-service layer — water refill, lactation, microwave, and zero-waste — exceeded the fetch tool’s response ceiling and came back silently truncated at 130 of 242 features, with one entire subtype missing. We only caught it by cross-checking the retrieved count against selfservice.features.length in the browser’s own JavaScript, which is authoritative because the browser had already downloaded and parsed the whole file to draw the map. The missing 112 features were recovered by querying that in-page object in batches.

Matching amenities to buildings

Each amenity was attached in two stages: nearest building within 45 m, and failing that, nearest building within 150 m that also matched on normalised name. Records satisfying neither were left out rather than guessed at — 281 attached, 37 held out. We validated by programmatic JSON well-formedness checks, by spot-checking buildings with independently verifiable ground truth (confirming a library’s lactation room, cafe, and water refill station all landed on the right record and not a visually adjacent one), and by manually reviewing the collision and held-out lists to confirm each was an expected case — adjacent sub-structures, off-campus sites, non-building waypoints — rather than a systematic failure.

Testing, and one thing we did not finish

Test results at submission.

Feature tests1513 Availability- and rating-based ordering not implemented.

One failure we did fix: cross-device persistence, which broke because saved data was not yet tied to authenticated accounts. Associating preferences with Firebase user IDs fixed it, verified by retesting on a second device. Stress testing also caught a mobile problem — the map’s minimum zoom stopped small-screen users from zooming out far enough to orient themselves — so we lowered it. On very small screens we hide the map entirely and keep search fully usable, protecting the primary workflow rather than degrading it.

We also wrote a full user manual covering account creation, every major workflow, expected results, and troubleshooting. Partly for grading, but mostly because writing it forces you to notice features that exist and cannot be found. Milestone 05 showed that concern was well-placed.

→ What this changed

Thirteen of fifteen feature tests passed. Both failures were the availability- and rating-based ordering we had committed to after the hi-fi critique — so we walked into user evaluation already knowing there was a gap between what we designed and what we shipped.

05

User evaluation

Six people, three tasks, and one very slow one

Participants finished 17 of 18 tasks. Finding an amenity was quick and clear. Contributing one was neither.

All six were UC Berkeley students and campus community members who regularly hunt for restrooms, water, and study space — the same population as our original needfinding — and none were enrolled in CS 160. We screen-recorded every session and measured completion, time, taps, and missteps against the same three tasks we have used since paper.

Mean completion time climbed from 30.2 s on Task 1 to 90.3 s on Task 3. Missteps followed exactly the same shape: 5, 5, then 14 — making contribution both the slowest and the most error-prone task, driven by sign-in and list navigation. Tap counts agree, though Task 3’s mean of 52.7 is skewed by a single session dominated by two-factor verification; its median of 19.5 is the honest figure.

Only one task failed outright. Participant 2 could not complete Task 1 — not because the answer was missing, but because it was in the wrong place. Their category search for “restroom” returned buildings about six miles away, while the genuinely nearest option, a gender-inclusive restroom 1.0 mile away at the Rogers Rowing Center, sat unclicked on the map with a low-contrast label at the edge of the frame. It was correctly listed under “Buildings Near You” the whole time.

The seven survey prompts
  1. Searching for and filtering nearby campus amenities (e.g. bathrooms, water fountains) was quick and intuitive.
  2. The interface made it easy to view and select nearby options.
  3. The detailed information (building, floor, written directions, photos) made it clear where to find the amenity within the building.
  4. Comparing different amenities to find the option that best fits my needs was straightforward.
  5. The process of submitting a review, photo, or issue report was clear and easy to complete.
  6. The overall design and layout of the app felt clean, clear, and visually appealing.
  7. If adopted by my campus community, I would regularly use this app to find and check amenities.

Most participants agreed that search was quick and intuitive, that the interface made options easy to compare, and that the detail we show is enough to actually find the amenity indoors. They disagreed about whether contributing was straightforward, and about whether they would use the app regularly.

Three themes from thinking aloud

  1. Unfiltered, alphabetical lists force manual scanning

    The problem is not confined to one screen. Any list long enough to need scanning becomes a bottleneck, and category search compounded it by ranking results that proximity ordering already had right — the failure behind our only incomplete task.

  2. Map entry points do not look interactive

    “I did not know that you could click on the names itself [of the buildings]… a little tutorial [would help].”

    Participant 3

    When search underperforms, nothing points users at the map as a fallback, so correct nearby answers go undiscovered. Meanwhile users elsewhere place unverified trust in our ordering:

    “I just want the nearest building, and I am assuming the buildings are ordered by how close they are to me, so I just pick the top building.”

    Participant 1

  3. The contribution loop is broken at both ends

    Sign-in creates friction and distrust at the moment someone wants to help, and the app misses the moment when it most needs data.

    “Why does this require me to log in? Fine, I’ll use my fake account…” — later: “It was functional, I just question the reason to collect my data information.”

    Participant 4

    That matters because reviews are the feature people said they wanted most: “what would matter to me the most is if people left reviews and that could help me determine a bit more about like what bathrooms are better.” On the other end, Participant 5 opened Hearst Field Annex A — a building with zero recorded amenities — and hit nothing at all, then proposed the fix themselves.

    “Clicking on a building that had no recorded amenities felt like a dead end… adding a quick way to report or add missing amenities directly on empty building pages would be a great addition.”

    Participant 5

→ What this changed

Every theme mapped onto a concrete change, and each of the five next steps below is tagged with the theme it answers.

06

Next steps

Five things we would do next

  1. Theme 1

    Live filtering and an A–Z index

    On the building and amenity-type lists, so long lists stop demanding a manual scan.

  2. Theme 2

    Fix category-search ranking

    Make it use the proximity data “Buildings Near You” already gets right — the study’s only task failure.

  3. Theme 2

    Make the map look tappable

    Auto-centre on nearby results, raise pin-label contrast, and add a one-time interactive hint.

  4. Theme 3

    Reduce contribution friction

    Allow anonymous or guest submissions — or at minimum, explain why sign-in is required at the moment it is asked for.

  5. Theme 3

    Turn empty buildings into prompts

    Replace the dead-end empty state with a contribution invitation, at exactly the moment the app most needs user input.

Closing

The same problem, five times, getting sharper

Across five milestones the throughline was that one problem kept resurfacing at every fidelity level, each time in a more specific form. Needfinding found that people cannot tell whether an amenity is currently usable. Paper testing found users questioning how reviews could be trusted and failing to find the contribution controls. The hi-fi critique asked us to put available amenities ahead of unavailable ones. And the final evaluation found that our shipped contribution flow was still the slowest, most error-prone, and least trusted part of the app — while the two availability features we promised after that critique had not been finished.

We do not read that as five failures. We read it as evidence that the earliest insight was the right one and it held: people need to trust an amenity’s status before they will commit to walking there. Closing that loop, in the data and in the interface, is the highest-leverage work left. The five next steps are scoped to exactly that unfinished thread.
